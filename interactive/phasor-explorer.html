Fluorescence lifetime: decay ↔ phasor

Fluorescence lifetime imaging records, at every pixel, how long molecules stay excited before they emit. That record can be read as a decay curve or as a single point on a phasor plot. This page puts the two side by side so you can see how one becomes the other.

Drag the sliders, or drag the points on the phasor plot. Everything updates as you move.

An idealized model. Two exponential components, an infinitely short laser pulse and perfect calibration. Real measurements also include the instrument response (IRF); phasor analysis removes it by calibrating against a reference dye of known lifetime.

1Two lifetimes, one phasor

Why phasors: each pixel's decay becomes one point, and mixtures follow simple geometry. A single-exponential decay always lands on the semicircle, sliding from (1, 0) toward the origin as the lifetime grows. A mix of two lifetimes lands on the straight line between them, and where it sits along that line is the fraction of photons from each: the lever rule.

2The laser sets the ruler

Why the repetition rate matters: the phasor is computed at the laser's modulation frequency, and that frequency decides which lifetimes the semicircle spreads out. Phase changes fastest with lifetime where ωτ = 1, about 2 ns at 80 MHz. Much shorter or much longer lifetimes crowd together at the ends of the semicircle. Higher harmonics move that sweet spot to shorter lifetimes.

Long lifetimes also leave light behind when the next pulse arrives. The phasor already includes that light because it is computed over the repeating signal; a fit that assumes each decay ends inside the window does not.

3Photons, noise and background

Why photon counts matter: each pixel's phasor is estimated from a limited number of photons, so real pixels scatter around the true point. The scatter shrinks with the square root of the photon count: four times the photons, half the spread. Uncorrelated background, such as detector dark counts or room light, adds photons that carry no timing. It pulls every phasor straight toward the origin, which leaves the phase lifetime unchanged but makes the modulation lifetime longer.
